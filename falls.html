Brief receive, drop and fall · no standing recovery

What happens?

This page reports the ten-episode full-play fall study, which is separate from the single missed-catch pair summarized on the main page. These are complete twelve second receiver plays: the machine throws, the robot attempts the receive, and the simulation continues through the landing. No initial tilt, push or pose teleport is added. The baseline briefly contacts the ball, drops it, falls and hits its head.

The visual balance abort and shallow brace are armed by the existing color and depth receiving cue at 4.06 s, then react to IMU tilt and angular velocity at 4.08 s. Both act before the baseline's first fingertip contact at 4.539 s and forfeit the catch. They are preemptive fall responses, not reactions to a contact oracle or confirmation that the ball was dropped.

The balance abort switches to the existing zero-velocity balance controller and remains standing without nonfoot floor contact. The shallow brace lands on the ground, settles and reduces peak head normal force from 1,183 to 77 N. Peak summed nonfoot normal force falls from 2,755 to 2,542 N; the largest 150 ms nonfoot normal impulse falls from 140.70 to 124.89 N·s. It does not stand up again.

Delaying the shallow brace until 4.68 s or 5.24 s still prevents head contact, but increases the overall peak impact. The 5.24 s response begins after the baseline loses bilateral contact at 4.823 s. These failed later responses show why the current benefit depends on acting early.

All variants

The deep tuck protects the head but worsens impact elsewhere. Reducing body stiffness to 50% or 25%, while retaining damping and the original torque caps, does not fix that tradeoff. The stiffness change ramps over 150 ms after the IMU trigger; native hand gains remain unchanged. Late balance aborts also fail to recover. Every variant is retained here.

An additional unarmed balance abort triggered during startup at 0.54 s and stopped the route before the throw. It is preserved as a false trigger example, not presented as a successful landing response.

Measurements and limits

The supervisor reads IMU, encoders and known commands. Visual arming uses the existing receiving phase command produced by fresh color and depth tracking. Exact body pose and floor forces are used only afterward for evaluation. No learned falling policy, contact force reward or ground recovery policy was trained. Existing locomotion is pretrained or scripted; its upstream training is a separate provenance limitation.

Each variant matches the fresh baseline exactly before its trigger in recorded joint positions, motion and ball contacts. This is a new reconstruction of a historical failed Go fixture, not an exact replay of its older archive. The pass starts at (0, 3.45, 1.40) m, a 3.45 m split from the machine to the receiver, not the current two-yard setup. The receiver drifts; this is not a newly qualified straight Go.

All clips use the same fixed camera and unchanged recorded poses. The green field and passing machine are presentation only; the field center is aligned with the surveyed machine. The loose ball may roll outside the frame after landing, while the receiver remains visible. All twelve seconds are included.

Normal forces are summed simulated floor contact forces, not calibrated injury estimates. “Other nonfoot” includes remaining leg and body surfaces, not soles. Maximum 150 ms impulse is the largest rolling integral of summed nonfoot normal force. The first 150 ms after the first small contact gives a different comparison: 29.20 N·s baseline versus 123.47 N·s early brace. We do not hide that window dependence.

“Settled on ground” requires continuous nonfoot floor support and low linear/angular velocity throughout the final two seconds. It does not mean standing recovery. Stable standing separately requires height >0.65 m, upright cosine >0.9, planar speed <0.15 m/s and angular speed <0.35 rad/s. These engineering definitions are not validated safety thresholds.

One prescribed throw and deterministic runs do not establish robust fall protection. Early stopping sacrifices the catch; later responses still have severe impacts. No hardware safety, learned recovery, novelty, contact material calibration or numerical convergence is claimed.
