Did reinforcement learning help?

In a separate Out experiment, PPO learned one bounded adjustment to reach speed. The visual trigger, receiving geometry, grip and locomotion remained scripted or separately pretrained. The final checkpoint trained for 30.1 minutes on an RTX PRO 6000, with 32,964 transitions and 149 completed episodes. Episodes were sampled from a 50/50 mixture of nominal and delayed/noisy camera domains; 71 of the 149 completed episodes used the noisy domain.

The final checkpoint increased nominal sensor reward from 0.0105 to 0.0279 but reduced physical two-hand control from 5 ms to 2 ms. None of the seven paired sensor-stress cases retained the ball at the endpoint. This policy was not promoted: higher reward alone was insufficient evidence of a better catch.

What can the robot actually observe?

Surveyed external color and depth cameras provide ball and receiver marker estimates. IMU and encoders supply orientation, angular velocity and joint state. An internal kinematic model uses those measurements to estimate the palms; it does not read the simulated robot’s exact pose. The latest Out observer aligns historical encoders and IMU with each camera exposure time and reports image age and uncertainty.

The new actor, critic and reward share this sensor boundary. They receive no contact forces, exact ball position, exact root state or future simulator trajectory. Simulator truth is used afterward for scoring and rendering. The pinned upstream AMP gait training configuration explicitly defines a privileged critic and simulator-state motion-discriminator features. Its exported actor uses IMU, joint and command inputs, but sensor-only runtime does not establish sensor-only training throughout. Privileged gait training is a known part of this project; runtime sensor availability and transfer are the requirements. Exact checkpoint training lineage is not fully established, and physical camera and hand integration is unverified.

The Go arm reach is triggered by a fresh visual time-to-contact estimate, with no absolute-time fallback. Native hand preload still uses a commanded machine release event. That event link, camera calibration and Aero hand channels require hardware integration. No onboard head camera or active gaze is established; the spectator camera is separate. LiDAR is not used.

A passive hypothetical torso/head housing color and depth camera sees all 205 sampled secure-hold frames in the Go, but none of 19 precontact frames. Its camera-to-palm transform uses a fixed mount and measured joint kinematics, not simulator root pose; all recorded physical arrays remain identical. It also sees the ball after mount support begins. After applying the detector’s existing depth-inlier filter, estimated mount clearances are only millimetres, while combined uncertainty is about 18 mm. The uncertainty intervals include contact in every tested frame. The diagnostic therefore abstains: visibility is not proof of legitimate support. This camera is not integrated hardware or active gaze.

Increasing resolution alone is not a monotonic improvement. With matching camera intrinsics and the same Out controller, 320×180, 640×360 and 848×480 give 1.785, 0.033 and 2.200 s of clean control. At 20 ms delay, the larger cameras give 1.895 and 2.744 s. All five trials fail the acquisition-form gate. Only the late-reach cameras change; the route and original grip cameras stay fixed. These are single fixed-fixture trials, not calibrated sensor benchmarks. All resolution measurements.

How are the experiments judged?

We preserve the original short-receive score and separately apply the predeclared anatomical audit: at least 0.75 s of continuous two-hand support, at least 90% bilateral contact in the final 0.25 s, no excluded body, floor or applied support, at most 10 mm ball overlap and 5 mm inter-hand overlap. The historical palm-body upward axis must have cosine ≥0.6 and stay within 0.15 m above the shoulders for at least 80% of acquisition samples. Actual receiving-pad facets are tilted relative to that historical body axis and are inspected separately. Upright balance, gait and reference-model identity are checked separately.

These are engineering gates, not validated injury or realism thresholds. The experimental contact model fails the reference-model check by design. We publish the original model failures, the longer-horizon failure, all sampled offsets, timestep sensitivity and unsuccessful learning results. Exact repeats establish reproducibility for one frozen environment, not statistical robustness.

The fall study compares ten complete receiver episodes in one shared field and machine scene. An early balance abort stays upright by forfeiting the catch; an early shallow brace lowers impact and settles on the ground. Later reactions worsen impact. The earlier 18 isolated tests remain available as supporting evidence. This ten-episode study is separate from the single missed-catch pair summarized on the main page. We do not claim a learned fall policy, ground-to-standing recovery, hardware safety or novelty.
